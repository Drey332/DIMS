List of Emergency Response Scenarios
Medical Emergencies: Serious illness, injury or fatality on site (including food poisoning cases requiring evacuation).
Fire Onboard: Fire or explosion on a vessel or offshore installation.
Collision: Vessel collision with another vessel or with an oilfield structure.
Grounding: Vessel grounding (running aground on seabed).
Oil Spill: Accidental release of fuel or oil to the sea.
Man Overboard (MOB): Person falling into the sea.
Missing Personnel: Unaccounted person on site (potentially overboard if not found).
Severe Weather: High winds, heavy seas or storm conditions endangering operations.
Helicopter Ditching: Crash or emergency landing of a helicopter offshore.
Security Threat: Piracy, armed attack, or other security incident (militancy, kidnapping) offshore.
Diving Emergencies:
Diver Injury/Unconscious: Injured or unresponsive diver underwater.
Decompression Illness (DCI): Diver suffering the bends or other barotrauma.
Diver Trapped/Entangled: Diver stuck on an object or inside a structure.
Umbilical Failure (Lost Diver): Loss of diver’s umbilical or communications (diver adrift on emergency gas).
Chamber Emergency: Fire or rapid depressurization in a deck decompression chamber or diving bell.

Grounding (Vessel Run Aground)
Bronze (Master/OSC): Immediate actions and assessment. If the vessel runs aground (hits the seabed or shoal), stop engines immediately to avoid worsening the damage. Sound the general alarm to muster crew and inform them of the situation. Assess damage: check all compartments for water ingress and inspect for hull breaches, especially around the bow and keel. Determine if the vessel is firmly aground or can be backed off. Location check: note the exact grounding position and nature of the ground (sand, mud, rocks) if known. If in an oilfield or sensitive area, notify the field control (e.g. “SPDC Marine Coordinator”) right away
file-kpzyxshakbx8fodmnjaere
 so they are aware of a potential hazard in the field.
Bronze (Master): Prevent worsening of situation. If taking on water, use ballast adjustments to stabilize the vessel (e.g. shift water to raise the grounded part if possible). Close watertight doors and valves to isolate any flooded sections. Sound tanks to see if any fuel tanks are breached – a spill risk. If fuel is at risk of leaking, deploy absorbent booms overboard if available, and notify Silver to get spill response ready. The Master should also consider the state of the tide: if high tide, the vessel may settle worse on low tide; if low tide, there may be an opportunity to refloat on the rising tide. Plan accordingly.
Silver (Emergency Coordinator): External notifications and assistance. Silver alerts relevant authorities of the grounding (Coast Guard, port authority if near shore) and the client/operator if in a field. Engage a salvage engineer or consult with vessel’s classification society for advice on refloating attempts. If the vessel is in a protected environmental zone, prepare oil spill response teams as a precaution. Silver might request tug support – if tugs are in reach, have them dispatched to assist in refloating or preventing further drift. Silver keeps Gold informed, especially if the grounding could become a high-profile incident (e.g. potential major pollution or traffic blockage).
Bronze (Master): Refloating efforts. Under guidance from Silver/experts, attempt to refloat the vessel if it’s safe: e.g. lighten the ship by pumping out ballast or transferring cargo (if possible) to reduce draft. If tide is rising, wait for sufficient water then use engines gently in combination with tugs or bow thrusters to maneuver off the ground. Ensure all crew are at safe stations during such attempts and monitor the hull for new flooding. Do not overstress the engines or hull; if it doesn’t free easily, stop to avoid damage and wait for salvage help.
Bronze/Silver: Prepare for abandonment or evacuation (if needed). Grounding itself might not demand abandoning ship unless the hull is badly damaged or the vessel is in immediate danger (e.g. breaking apart or at risk of capsizing). Continuously evaluate stability – if the vessel lists heavily or pounding waves threaten her integrity, the Master and Silver should prepare evacuation plans. Muster crew with lifejackets and launch boats only if absolutely required. Typically, it’s safer to stay on a grounded vessel unless she’s sinking, but be ready. Silver would coordinate SAR helicopters or nearby vessels to remove crew if abandonment is ordered.
Gold (Strategic): Strategic management and communication. Gold will handle company-level issues: notifying insurers (hull and P&I clubs) of a potential claim, assembling a salvage contract (likely LOF – Lloyd’s Open Form – or similar if needed), and informing senior stakeholders. Gold manages media inquiries if the grounding becomes public (to control any reputational impact). If the grounding happens in international waters or foreign territory, Gold ensures legal and diplomatic protocols are observed (through the flag state or agents). Gold also ensures that environmental authorities are engaged if there's pollution risk.
Follow-up: Once refloated or situation contained, the vessel should proceed to a safe port for thorough inspection. Bronze and Silver coordinate temporary repairs or escort tugs for the transit if hull strength is in question. An internal investigation should be launched (usually Gold will oversee this later) to find the cause of the grounding. All reports (to authorities, insurers, client) must be completed by Silver, with Gold reviewing for accuracy.
(Roles: Bronze is Master on scene managing damage control and navigation; Silver is onshore coordinator arranging tugs, salvage, notifications; Gold handles high-level incident management, external communications, and support.)

Bronze (Dive Supervisor/LST): Recognize bell separation and stabilize situation. A “lost bell” means the closed diving bell has inadvertently detached from its tether or umbilicals. The Life Support Technician (LST) in the bell or on surface should ensure the bell’s internal* life support is activated – switch to onboard oxygen supply, scrubbers, and clamp shut any leaking valves. The bell occupants should lock the hatch and stay calm on internal systems.
Bronze (Master/Dive Sup): Locate the bell. Initiate tracking – most closed bells have an acoustic locator beacon. Use the vessel’s bell tracking sonar or a handheld pinger receiver to get a bearing and distance to the bell
law.resource.org
. Mark the position (DGPS) where the bell was lost and note time and currents. Immediately maneuver the vessel (or assign a standby vessel/ROV) to search above the bell’s last known location. Deploy an ROV if available to visually locate the bell under water.
Bronze (Dive Team): Prepare for bell recovery. Get the bell recovery equipment ready: this may include a standby ROV to hook onto the bell’s lifting eye, a dedicated “bell recovery winch” or davit, and extra divers if shallow enough for surface divers to assist. Ensure the hyperbaric rescue chamber (if part of system) is ready to receive divers if the bell is brought to surface and mating is needed. Maintain communication with the bell via emergency channel if possible – talk to the divers to reassure them and get their condition.
Silver (Onshore Dive Manager): Mobilize external resources. Lost bell scenarios are dire; Silver should notify any nearby dive support vessels (DSVs) or rescue assets in the region. IOGP guidance stresses having contingency plans and possibly shared resources for bell recovery
law.resource.org
. If the company has a submarine rescue or a backup vessel, put them on standby. Also notify the client and possibly the government/navy – they may have heavy lifting or ROV assets to help. Silver coordinates the overall search-and-recovery efforts, allowing the vessel crew (Bronze) to focus on immediate tasks.
Gold (Strategic): High-level emergency management. Gold convenes the full emergency team and possibly an industry mutual aid, as saturation divers’ lives are at extreme risk. Gold may trigger the company’s hyperbaric rescue agreement (if one exists with a rescue company or neighboring operator). Ensure that a hyperbaric rescue facility is ready at a safe location to receive the bell or the divers after recovery (this could be a known safe refuge as required by IOGP
scribd.com
). Gold coordinates media and stakeholder communications, as a lost bell will draw significant attention. They also ensure regulatory notifications are immediate (e.g. the HSE or relevant offshore safety body).
Bronze/Recovery Team: Perform bell recovery. Once the bell is located, use ROV or suitable means to attach the recovery line to the bell’s lifting point. If the original handling system is still usable (e.g. cable intact but snagged), carefully work it free; otherwise, use an auxiliary winch. Winch the bell up slowly, monitoring bell integrity and ensuring it doesn’t tilt excessively. Once near surface or at mating depth, transfer the bell to the deck or mating trunk as per your system. The LST and medics should be standing by – do not open the bell until it’s properly mated or in a controlled environment (to avoid dropping pressure if divers are under pressure). If an SPHL (self-propelled hyperbaric lifeboat) was the lost bell, locate and retrieve it similarly, then connect to deck chamber.
Aftermath: When the divers are safe and transferred to a chamber or on deck, ensure they get medical checks – they may have CO₂ exposure, anxiety issues, etc. They will likely need to undergo normal decompression unless an emergency ascent was done. Silver and Gold should then focus on investigating the cause of the bell loss (wire failure? clump weight issue? human error?) and reporting to industry bodies (IMCA safety alert perhaps). The effectiveness of the contingency plan
law.resource.org
 will be reviewed and improved as needed.
(Roles: Bronze (on vessel Dive Sup/Master) run the immediate tracking and recovery rigging; Silver orchestrates additional help and ensures nothing is missing; Gold manages the critical crisis at the organizational level, given the life-or-death stakes and potential for wide impact.)
Chamber Emergency (Fire or Rapid Depressurization in DDC)
Bronze (LST/Dive Supervisor): React to chamber fire. A fire in the decompression chamber is an extremely urgent situation – the chamber atmosphere can fuel fire intensely. If smoke or fire is detected in a chamber: immediately cut power to chamber electrics (many systems have an emergency shutoff) to remove ignition sources
law.resource.org
. Initiate the chamber’s built-in fire suppression if available (CO₂ dump system into the chamber). Chamber occupants should be instructed (via intercom or banging on hull) to don emergency breathing masks (EBAs) which supply fresh air/oxygen from outside sources
law.resource.org
. Do not open the chamber to atmosphere while at pressure – that could cause catastrophic decompression and feed the fire with oxygen. Instead, fight the fire internally: if occupants are conscious, have them use onboard fire extinguishers (often chambers have internal handheld extinguishers suitable for use at pressure).
Bronze (LST/Dive Tech): Handle rapid depressurization. If the chamber suddenly loses pressure (due to a leak or accidental door opening), act to contain the pressure drop: close any reachable valves, isolate the chamber if it’s part of a system (close compartment valves). If pressure loss cannot be stopped, it will result in an emergency surfacing of any divers inside – prepare to treat those divers for severe DCI and barotrauma. As soon as the chamber can be opened (when equalized with ambient), have medical personnel ready to render aid. If a bell loses pressure underwater (e.g. hatch inadvertently opened or seal failure), instruct the bell divers via comms to get on their back-up breathing gas and try to close/secure the source of leak; bring bell to surface if possible. In all cases, prioritize life over assets – a controlled emergency ascent is preferable to asphyxiation.
Bronze (Master/OSC): General vessel response. In case of chamber fire especially, treat it as a shipboard fire too: sound the alarm, muster fire teams to the chamber area with firefighting gear. They must be cautious of using water on a chamber (could crack hot metal) – better to use dry powder or CO₂ extinguishers around the outside and cool the area. Ensure ventilation to the compartment housing the chamber is shut down (to starve fire of O₂). If chamber is venting smoke from relief valves, vent it overboard via duct if possible.
Silver (Dive/Project Manager): Medical and technical backup. Get hyperbaric medical experts on the line immediately – for guidance on managing chamber emergencies
mds.uy
. For a fire, once out, the occupants may have burns or smoke inhalation; plan for their evacuation to a hospital with a specialized burns or lung treatment facility. For a blow-up (rapid decompression), multiple divers could have severe DCI: coordinate multiple recompression facilities if one chamber is out of commission. Essentially, implement the contingency for recompression at an alternate site since the primary chamber is compromised
scribd.com
. This might mean mobilizing the injured by helicopter to the nearest dive chamber (even if in another country, time is critical). Silver also alerts Gold about the severity (these are likely to be recordable accidents).
Gold: Major incident management. Gold will oversee this as a serious incident – possibly multiple casualties or a near-miss fatal. They will engage all needed external support: manufacturers (if technical advice needed on chamber issues), additional hyperbaric experts, and communications to authorities. If a fatality or hospitalization occurs, Gold handles official notifications and media statements. They ensure full support to the on-site team who will be shaken by such an event, and might decide to halt all project operations until safety can be assured.
Outcome: Once the immediate emergency is over (fire out or chamber stabilized at ambient), secure the area. Do not use the chamber again until a thorough investigation and recertification. Treat and evacuate all injured. Later, investigate root causes: e.g. was it an electrical fault that caused fire (IMCA requires no ignition sources in chamber beyond minimal)? Was there a procedural failure leading to rapid decompression (human error on door closure)? Implement improved controls. This scenario being realized is extremely hazardous; learning from it is crucial for industry guidance updates.
(Roles: Bronze (LST/Dive Supervisor and vessel crew) handle direct firefighting or containment; Silver coordinates medical response and alternate arrangements for pressure-related treatment; Gold manages overall emergency and aftermath at company level.)
Hyperbaric Evacuation (Saturation Dive Rescue)
Bronze (Master/Dive Sup): Initiate hyperbaric rescue mode. If the vessel must be abandoned or cannot support life support due to a major emergency (sinking, massive fire) while divers are under saturation, you must launch the self-propelled hyperbaric lifeboat (SPHL) or hyperbaric rescue capsule. Sound the abandon ship for diving crew specifically and get all saturation divers into the designated evacuation chamber (if they aren’t already in the living chamber, they may need to be transferred under pressure to the SPHL). Ensure the SPHL’s life support umbilicals are disconnected and its internal systems activated (scrubbers, oxygen supply). Launch the SPHL from the vessel as per procedure – usually by free-fall or crane – ensuring it clears the vessel safely
law.resource.org
. If using a portable HRU (Hyperbaric Rescue Unit) that must be craned off, do so as early as possible in the emergency, when crane power is still available.
Bronze (SPHL Crew): Navigate to safe area. The SPHL, once waterborne with divers inside, should move away from the danger area (e.g. if the main vessel is on fire or sinking). Typically a trained coxswain inside the SPHL will navigate it. The craft’s first objective is to reach the pre-designated safe refuge – this might be a nearby platform, another vessel, or a mother ship that can receive it
law.resource.org
. If no propulsion, the SPHL will rely on currents or rescue vessels to tow it. The occupants will maintain themselves – they have minimum 24 hours of life support onboard by design
law.resource.org
.
Silver (Onshore Dive/Marine Coordinator): Guide rescue vessel to lifeboat and alert receiving facility. As soon as a hyperbaric evacuation is launched, Silver notifies the standby rescue vessel (or any nearby ship designated to pick up the lifeboat). Provide coordinates or tracking info for the SPHL. Arrange for a lift onto a support vessel or tow as needed. Critically, alert the Hyperbaric Reception Facility – as per IOGP 478, a shoreside (or ship-based) facility should be prepared to take the SPHL and transfer divers under pressure for controlled decompression
iogp.org
. Silver coordinates with that facility (could be a specialized deck chamber system on a quayside) to be ready at the time the SPHL arrives. Also coordinate medical support in case any diver’s health is deteriorating inside (they might have trauma or panic issues).
Gold (Strategic): Global emergency support. A hyperbaric evacuation is a rare and critical event. Gold will notify other authorities (coast guard, naval SAR) that a special rescue operation is underway – this often grants priority assistance. They will likely engage the company’s global resources – possibly flying in additional saturation experts or connecting with the equipment manufacturer for technical help on the transfer. Gold also manages corporate communications: a sat diver evacuation will draw industry attention; they must communicate carefully once divers are safe, and not before (to avoid premature information). Family members of the divers will need personal assurance and updates, which Gold/HR handles.
Rescue & Transfer: The rescue vessel retrieves the SPHL. Ideally, it has a mating trunk or cradle to secure the SPHL on deck. Once stable, do not open the SPHL. Instead, transfer the SPHL (still sealed) to the prepared reception facility. This often involves lifting the SPHL onto a truck or directly mating to a deck chamber system depending on logistics. Maintain pressure and life support throughout. The divers will then undergo a normal decompression schedule in the safe location, overseen by hyperbaric doctors. This decompression could take days – ensure security and support for them throughout.
Follow-up: After the divers are safely decompressed and out, Gold and Silver focus on the primary incident that caused the evacuation (the vessel emergency) and also thoroughly review the hyperbaric evacuation process effectiveness. This is typically reviewed by industry bodies too (IOGP etc., per Report 478 recommendations)
iogp.org
. All equipment used must be inspected and any shortcomings fixed. The fact that this scenario was executed means the contingency planning was validated, but any hiccups (communication issues, delays) should be learned from and documented.
(Roles: Bronze (dive/vessel crew) carry out the physical evacuation of sat divers; Silver orchestrates the rendezvous and receiving facilities; Gold handles the high-level crisis management and ensures no resource is spared in bringing the divers home safely.)
Contaminated Diver (Diving in Hazardous Waters)
Bronze (Dive Supervisor): Identify contamination and isolate diver. If a diver reports exposure to a hazardous substance (chemicals, hydrocarbons, H₂S) or dives in known contaminated water, special procedures apply. Before the diver surfaces, have the deck team ready at the designated decontamination station (often a specific area on deck). If the contaminant is toxic gas like H₂S, ensure everyone handling the diver is in proper PPE (SCBA if high levels). Do not bring the diver into the normal personnel areas until decontaminated – this may mean keeping their hat/gear on and washing down externally first.
Bronze (Dive Team): Decontamination process. Use copious amounts of water (preferably warm, with appropriate detoxifying agent if known) to rinse the diver’s suit and gear while still on the dive stage or at water level. For example, for oil-based contaminants, use degreaser or detergent in the wash. Pay attention to areas around seals, boots, and hands. Once gross decon is done, carefully remove the diver’s helmet and suit, avoiding contact with their skin and avoiding exposing support crew to contaminants (use gloves, etc.). Place contaminated gear into sealed bags or barrels for later cleaning/disposal.
Bronze (Medic/DMT): Assess the diver’s health. If the diver ingested or inhaled something (e.g. chemical fumes or H₂S), move them to fresh air and administer oxygen as a precaution. Remove any clothing and further decon their body (shower the diver) if there was skin contact with a toxic substance. Some chemicals can cause burns or systemic toxicity – check the diver for any burns, dizziness, breathing difficulty. The medic should contact the poison control center or hyperbaric doctor with details of the exposure for specific first aid advice
scribd.com
.
Silver (HSE Manager/EC): Notify and seek specialist help. Report the incident to onshore HSE and, if needed, call in a specialist hazmat team (for example, if the contaminant is unknown or extremely dangerous). Silver should have the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) of the suspected contaminant if known (e.g. if diving near an oil platform, substances like methane, benzene, or production chemicals might be known hazards). Provide this to medical personnel. Also notify the client/operator since contaminated water incidents might indicate a leak (for example, if divers encountered an unexpected chemical, the source might be a pipeline leak that the client needs to address).
Gold: Oversight and external reporting. Gold monitors the diver’s medical status – if hospital care is needed, ensure a medevac is arranged. For example, H₂S poisoning might require hyperbaric oxygen therapy or simply critical care if severe. Gold also ensures any regulatory reporting is done (environmental release, recordable injury, etc.). If multiple crew were potentially exposed (say through handling the diver before realizing contamination), manage that by medical checks for all.
Follow-up: Treat all used decon water and materials as hazardous waste – dispose of according to regulations (client can assist if on their site). Investigate how the exposure happened: Did risk assessment foresee this? Were proper protective measures in place (was the diver in a hot-water suit or splash suit that should protect from contaminants? Was monitoring for H₂S in place?)
scribd.com
. Update procedures to ensure future dives in such conditions have better controls (e.g. use of surface-supplied umbilicals with gas monitoring, standby with EBA for diver, etc.). If the contaminant was from the environment (like a pollution event), it may trigger an environmental response parallel to this – which Silver/Gold would coordinate with relevant parties.
(Roles: Bronze handles on-site decon and immediate care; Silver engages necessary onshore resources and experts; Gold ensures strategic response including medical evacuation and compliance.)
Critical Equipment Failure (DP Failure / Power Loss during Diving)
Bronze (Master/Dive Supervisor): Immediate dive safety actions. If the vessel’s dynamic positioning (DP) system fails or power is lost while divers are in the water, there is a risk the vessel will drift off station. The Bridge should inform the Dive Supervisor immediately of the failure. The Dive Supervisor must then execute emergency diver retrieval: instruct the diver to leave the work and start ascending immediately (or head to the bell). If a bell is in use, the bellman should secure the diver back into the bell quickly. If it’s a surface-supplied dive with no bell, the diver should begin a controlled ascent (hopefully with umbilical being hauled in by the deck crew). In a power loss, the winches might not work, so be prepared to manually heave umbilicals. Use the emergency recovery winch if available (air powered). The goal is to get the diver to surface or bell before the vessel moves significantly from above them.
Bronze (Master): Maintain position if possible. On the bridge, engage backup DP systems (emergency DP, or manual thruster control) to keep position. If total power loss, immediately drop an anchor (if depth allows and you have quick-release system) to create some holding, provided it doesn’t risk the diver. If multiple thrusters failed but some propulsion remains, try to create minimal movement. Also, inform the diver via through-water comms (if umbilical comms lost with DP) that DP is lost and to brace for potential pull or slack – this may psychologically prepare them.
Bronze (Deck Crew): Umbilical management. The dive team should be ready to manually shear the diver’s umbilical if the vessel is about to capsize or drag the diver – as a last resort, to let the diver free (they would then resort to bailout gas and free ascent). This is extremely dangerous, but better than dragging a diver by a moving vessel. Typically, quick-release fittings or axes are on hand for this reason. Stay in constant contact with Bridge to know if a drive-off is occurring.
Silver (Onshore): Support restoration or rescue. Silver is notified of the critical failure. They might coordinate an additional support vessel to assist or to take over holding station above divers if possible. If the divers made it to bell or surface safely but the vessel is now incapacitated, Silver will arrange for tow or repair crew. If a diver was injured or separated in the chaos, treat as a diver missing or injured scenario concurrently.
Gold: High-level response if incident escalates. If the DP failure led to an actual incident (diver stranded, bell on seabed without ship above, or vessel collision), Gold steps in to handle that multi-faceted emergency (some aspects covered in previous scenarios). If not, Gold will at least be involved in investigating the DP failure cause and ensuring operations do not resume until safety is assured.
Outcome: Ideally, the diver is recovered to surface or bell without injury. The dive may have to be aborted and divers put through a shortened deco or surface decompression due to the emergency ascent – ensure proper DCI monitoring after. If the diver did an emergency ascent (skipping deco), put them on O₂ and into chamber for treatment table as a precaution. Only once power/DP is fully restored and an investigation is done should diving resume. The cause of DP failure (human error, tech fault) should be identified by Silver/Gold with the marine team, and any necessary changes made (e.g. more redundancy or different weather limits).
(Roles: Bronze (Master & Dive Sup) act immediately to save the diver’s life and stabilize vessel position; Silver provides external support and coordination after the initial emergency; Gold oversees the investigation and any compounded emergencies as a result of the failure.)
